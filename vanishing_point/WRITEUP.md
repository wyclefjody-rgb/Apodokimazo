# Where does Paul's knowledge stop? The Daniel method applied to Paul

## Why Daniel

Most critical scholars hold that the Book of Daniel was written around 167-164 BCE but set in 6th-century
Babylon. The ancient critic Porphyry argued this in the late 3rd century CE (his argument survives through
Jerome's commentary on Daniel). What exposed it was mostly content, not style:
- **Its knowledge stops at a point.** The "prophecy" of Daniel 11 is exact up to the persecution under Antiochus
  IV, then predicts his death wrongly.
- **It slips about its own setting.** Belshazzar is made king and Nebuchadnezzar's son, and a "Darius the Mede"
  rules Babylon.
- **It has late words.** Greek names for musical instruments appear in Daniel 3.

Nina Livesey argues that the seven letters usually held to be Paul's were written about 144 CE, in Marcion's school
in Rome, after the Bar Kokhba revolt (132-135). If so, they were written about ninety years after the time they
describe. So the same questions apply: where does their knowledge stop, and do they slip?

## The method, and whether it works

Two parts:
- **Part A:** counts, by code, of marks of 2nd-century church life in every early Christian text of the kit: the
  name "Christian", a single bishop over a church, elders as an office, written gospels cited as books, and the
  "in appearance only" dispute.
- **Part B:** every datable passage, read and coded "before 70", "after 70/135" or "either", with its strength and
  a reason.

Controls check the method can find lateness when it is there:
- 2 Peter and the Pastorals, which most critical scholars judge written after their setting;
- 1 Clement, Ignatius, Polycarp, the Didache and Justin;
- the Ascension of Isaiah, added at your request (it survives in Ethiopic, so it is coded from translations only).

**The method passes.**
- **2 Peter** has two strong later features: mockers ask where the promised coming is "since the fathers fell
  asleep" (3:4), and Paul's letters are a collection read "as the other scriptures" (3:15-16).
- **The Pastorals** have two medium ones: a gospel saying (Luke 10:7) cited as "scripture" in 1 Timothy 5:18, and
  a settled church order with enrolled widows and appointed elders.
- **Justin**, a Roman Christian of the 150s, names "the recent Jewish war" of Bar Kokhba and says Jews may no
  longer go up to Jerusalem. That is exactly the kind of slip a 144 date for Paul would lead one to look for.

The four items first coded "before 70, strong" were recoded "either" on review, because none is decisive without
independent corroboration and a later writer could have set each in the past:
1. **King Aretas' governor at Damascus** (2 Corinthians 11:32). Aretas IV died in 40. The detail is not in Acts,
   but a later writer could have had another source.
2. **"Most of whom are still alive"** (1 Corinthians 15:6), which could be the story's time, not the writer's.
3. **The end expected within the writer's lifetime** (1 Thessalonians 4:15; 1 Corinthians 15:51; 7:29; Romans
   13:11), which could be kept as part of the apostle's voice.
4. **A working Jerusalem church receiving a collection** (1 Corinthians 16; 2 Corinthians 8-9; Romans 15), which
   could be set in the past.

The one strong candidate for a late slip in the undisputed letters is 1 Thessalonians 2:16 ("wrath has come upon
them at last"). It sits in a passage many scholars already think was added later, and some read it of an earlier
disaster. Galatians 4:25 ("the present Jerusalem is in slavery") could be read of Jerusalem after 135, but its own
allegory explains it, so it is coded "either".

Two items bear on Livesey's particular setting:
- **The circumcision dispute.** She ties the letters' circumcision debate to the time after Bar Kokhba, when Rome
  may have forbidden circumcision (Historia Augusta, Hadrian 14.2; the ban itself is doubted). But in Galatians
  the pressure runs the other way: other believers press gentile converts *to be* circumcised, "so that they may
  not be persecuted for the cross" (Galatians 6:12). That fits a time when the danger came from fellow Jews,
  better than one when Rome punished circumcision.
- **Marcion** (revised, AMENDMENTS.md 4). Marcion held that the God of Israel's scriptures, the Creator, was not
  the Father of Jesus. He did not reject those scriptures as false (Litwa); he denied that they speak of the Father.
  The catholic Romans calls the law "holy", insists God has not rejected Israel and quotes Israel's scriptures
  throughout. But Tertullian says Marcion's own Romans lacked most of chapters 9-11, so that part is no evidence
  about Marcion's text. What did stand in it is tested directly in marcion_text (Part B): Tertullian attests 17
  passages in Marcion's own edition that speak against Marcion's theology on their face, including "the law is
  holy" (Romans 7:12).

## What this does and does not show

- **It shows no late slip.** Nothing in the core four fits only after 70 or after 135, and they lack every mark
  of 2nd-century church life that the same counts find in Ignatius, the Didache and Justin. The same method does
  find later features in 2 Peter, the Pastorals and Justin. That is the half of the Daniel test that can be run
  without outside evidence, and the letters pass it.
- **It does not show an early date.** Under the standard that an internal detail needs independent
  corroboration, nothing in the letters pins them before 70. The remaining "before 70" items are medium or weak.
- **So the test is one-sided.** Daniel was exposed by what it got wrong about its own setting. The core four get
  nothing wrong that the test can see; but a careful writer of 144 who avoided every slip would also pass. The
  method narrows Livesey's hypothesis (such writers would have had to avoid everything 2nd-century writers we can
  check did not avoid) but cannot rule it out.

## Limits

- Every code is a judgment. Each is written down with its reason in CHECKLIST.md so it can be disputed. They were
  made by Claude (an AI model) and are being reviewed by a person; the strong items have been reviewed, the medium
  and weak ones not yet.
- Some dates come only from Acts or from later writers; those items are marked "weak" or "either".
- Present-tense Temple language is left out of the count because its value for dating is disputed.
- The Ascension of Isaiah is coded from translations and scholarship, not from its text in the kit.
