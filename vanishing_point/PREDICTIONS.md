# The Daniel method applied to Paul: predictions and rules

Written 2026-10-23, before the evidence below was collected or coded. Never edited; changes go in AMENDMENTS.md
with a date. This study is not blind: the main datable passages of Paul are well known, and some were named in
the conversation that led to it. What is fixed here is how they are judged, and which control texts must behave.

## 1. The question and the method

Daniel was shown to be a 2nd-century BCE book set in the 6th century by its "vanishing point": its knowledge is
exact up to the persecution under Antiochus IV and then fails, and it contains slips (people, titles, loanwords)
that belong to the writer's own time, not to the story's.

Nina Livesey (The Letters of Paul in Their Roman Literary Context, 2024) argues that the seven letters usually
held to be Paul's were composed about 144 CE in a Roman school setting, most likely Marcion's, by several writers,
after the Bar Kokhba revolt (132-135); she also dates 1 Clement, Ignatius, Polycarp's letter and 2 Peter to 140-150.

If she is right, the letters were written about 90 years after their story is set. The Daniel method asks: where
does their knowledge stop, and do they slip?

## 2. Texts

Test texts: Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians (the core four), with Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon
reported separately.
Control texts, which the method must handle correctly for its answer on Paul to count:
- known or widely agreed pseudonymous letters written after their story's setting: 2 Peter, 1 and 2 Timothy,
  Titus (the method should find later features in them);
- early Christian texts usually dated c. 95-160: 1 Clement, Ignatius, Polycarp, Didache, Justin (the method should
  find features of their own time in them; Livesey's own dates for some of them are reported alongside).

## 3. What is checked

Part A (counted by code, every text of the lineup study written by Christians, per 10,000 words; every hit listed):
- A1 the names "Christian" and "Christianity" (Χριστιανός, Χριστιανισμός)
- A2 a single bishop over a church (ἐπίσκοπος in the singular, used of a church office)
- A3 elders as a church office (πρεσβύτερος / πρεσβυτέριον in a church setting)
- A4 written gospels cited as books ("the gospel" as a text quoted or read; "memoirs of the apostles")
- A5 "appearance only" language about Jesus' suffering (τὸ δοκεῖν), the docetic dispute
A2-A5 need reading in context; the code finds candidates and each is coded by hand with a reason.

Part B (read and coded by hand, with sources): every passage in the test and control texts that can be tied to a
datable person, office, event, expectation or institution. Each is coded:
- "only before 70": fits the 30s-60s and would be an anachronism, an embarrassment or pointless invention by 144;
- "only after 70" / "only after 135": fits only a later time (a slip, in Daniel's sense);
- "either": fits both, or a later writer could easily have added it as setting.
Each "only" call states its evidence and how strong it is. Passages widely argued to be later additions (1
Corinthians 14:34-35, 2 Corinthians 6:14-7:1, 1 Thessalonians 2:13-16) are coded but reported apart.

## 4. Decision rules

- The method works if each pseudonymous control (2 Peter, the Pastorals) shows at least one "only after 70" item.
  If it does not, the method cannot see lateness and no conclusion about Paul is drawn.
- Daniel-type evidence for a late Paul: at least one "only after 70" item in the core four outside the passages
  argued to be additions.
- Evidence for an early Paul: "only before 70" items in the core four, weighed by how hard they would be for a
  writer of 144 to produce, and by whether they show the kind of failed expectation that exposed Daniel.
- The Part A counts are descriptions: they show where markers of 2nd-century church life appear.

## 5. Predictions

1. The method works: 2 Peter and the Pastorals each show at least one "only after 70" item (2 Peter: Paul's letters
   as scripture and the delayed coming; the Pastorals: a settled order of bishop, elders and enrolled widows).
2. The core four show no "only after 70" item outside the passages argued to be additions.
3. 1 Thessalonians 2:16 ("wrath has come upon them at last") is the one strong candidate for an "only after 70"
   item in the undisputed letters, and it lies inside a passage argued to be an addition.
4. The core four show several "only before 70" items, the strongest being living leaders in Jerusalem (Cephas,
   James, John), a functioning Jerusalem church receiving a collection, and the expectation that the end would come
   within the writer's own lifetime, an expectation that by 144 had visibly failed.
5. Part A: "Christian(s)", a single bishop and written gospels appear in Ignatius, the Didache, Justin and other
   2nd-century texts, and not in the core four.
6. Overall: the letters' knowledge does not stop at 135 or at 70 in the way Daniel's stops at 165 BCE; it fits a
   writer of the 50s. This cannot exclude a writer of 144 who avoided every slip, but it puts the burden of
   explanation on that hypothesis.

## 6. Limits stated in advance

- A careful writer can avoid slips; the method can show slips, not prove their absence means authenticity.
- Many items depend on Acts or on later writers for their dates; where a date rests only on Acts it is marked.
- Present-tense mentions of the Temple are weak evidence: 1 Clement and Josephus also speak of Temple worship in
  the present tense after 70.
- The control texts' own dates are debated; 2 Peter and the Pastorals are chosen because most critical scholars
  judge them later than their setting (some conservative scholars do not), and the other controls because they are
  usually dated by evidence that does not depend on Paul.
